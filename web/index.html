Derivation

Show the integrated rate law

Assumptions. Constant volume, a single reactant, isothermal operation, no reverse reaction, and rate = k[A]n.

Symbols. C₀ initial concentration (M); X conversion (dimensionless); t time; n order (dimensionless); k the rate constant, whose units are concentration1−n × time−1 and therefore change with the order.

1. Separate variables. From −dC/dt = kCn, collect C on one side and t on the other.

2. Integrate from C₀ to C. With C = C₀(1−X) this gives t = C₀1−n[(1−X)1−n − 1] / [(n−1)k], for every order except one.

3. The case n = 1, separately. The general result divides by (1−n), which is undefined at n = 1, because the integral of 1/C is a logarithm rather than a power. There the answer is t = −ln(1−X)/k.

Continuity. As n approaches 1 the general expression converges on the logarithmic one, so the two cases agree in the limit and the split is an artefact of how the integral is written, not a discontinuity in the chemistry.

What this means. One measured point fixes k for a chosen order, and k then fixes the whole curve. Move the order slider and watch every read-out change together.

What a rate law is, and where order comes from

A rate law relates how fast a reaction runs to how much reactant is present: rate = k[A]n. The exponent n is the reaction order.

Order comes from experiment, not the balanced equation. 2A → B is not automatically second order [3].

From the differential form to the integrated form

The differential form says how fast things are changing right now. The integrated form says where you will be at a given time. Getting from one to the other is the step textbooks most often leave implicit [5], so the Estimate page shows it in full under Derivation.

Order by order

Same half life, five orders. Low orders finish; high orders flatten.

Equal steps every time is the first order fingerprint. It is not true of decay in general [4].
Before you check: at zero order, is the second half life longer than, the same as, or shorter than the first?
Shorter, by half. At zero order the rate is constant, so equal times remove equal amounts of material. The first half life consumes half of C₀; the second has only half as much left to get through, so it takes half as long. Set the order slider to 0 on Estimate and read the annotations.

Why one point is not enough

A single measurement fixes one point on the curve. It does not fix the order.

Temperature

The Arrhenius equation gives k(T₂)/k(T₁) = exp[−Ea/R · (1/T₂ − 1/T₁)].

Heating does not lower the barrier. It raises the fraction of collisions that clear the same barrier [3].

"The rate roughly doubles every ten degrees" is a rule of thumb, not a law. It holds near Ea ≈ 53 kJ/mol at room temperature; at 100 kJ/mol the same ten-kelvin step is a factor of 3.7. The per-ten-degree factor shown on Estimate is computed from your current activation energy rather than asserted.

One honest caveat: a straight line on an Arrhenius plot is not on its own evidence that the activation energy is temperature independent [18]. Over a narrow range many non-Arrhenius models also look straight.

Before you check: on a logarithmic time axis, raising the temperature shifts the 90% point left. What happens to the 50% point?
By exactly the same amount. The shift is log10(k₂/k₁) decades at every conversion level and for every order, so the curve translates rigidly. Switch the axis to Log time on Estimate and drag the predicted temperature to watch it slide.

Fitting data honestly

Fit the untransformed data. Linearizing a rate law and running least squares on the transformed variables distorts the error structure and systematically flatters whichever model the transform suits. In a reanalysis of 225 published kinetic datasets, the linearized form preferred the pseudo-second-order model in every single case, while nonlinear fitting of the same data preferred pseudo-first-order in 54% of them [12]. The same conclusion holds across simulated error structures [13][14] and in enzyme kinetics [15].

Judge a fit by the structure in its residuals, not by R². A high R² is compatible with an obviously wrong order; a residual plot that curves is not.

Worked example with published numbers

Bleaching of the food dye Allura Red by hypochlorite, followed with a smartphone camera, is first order in the dye and gives an activation energy of 44.55 kJ/mol from a two-point Arrhenius treatment [10]. It is real, sourced, and reproducible at home, which makes it a better default than an invented number.
